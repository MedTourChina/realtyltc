:::answer
Both transactions pay deed tax at the same rates. After that they diverge: VAT and individual income tax exist only on resale and are legally the seller's, but market convention in most Chinese cities makes the buyer pay them. New builds instead carry a maintenance fund, a year of property fees up front, and a settlement for any difference between the contracted and surveyed floor area. Compare them at the same price and the resale usually costs more in cash on completion — and more again if the bank values it below what you agreed to pay.
:::

## Two transactions that only look alike

The mistake is to compare the two on price per square metre and treat the fees as noise. On a five-million-yuan flat the difference between the two routes is typically a few hundred thousand, and almost all of it sits in four lines that a first-time foreign buyer has never seen.

Here is the full structure, before any negotiation.

## What both pay: deed tax

契税 (qìshuì, deed tax) is paid by the buyer, on both new and resale, at the same rate. It is assessed on the transaction price or the tax authority's assessed value, whichever is higher — which matters when a price is deliberately written low.

| Household position | Floor area | Rate |
| --- | --- | --- |
| First home for the household | 140 m² or less | 1% |
| First home for the household | Over 140 m² | 1.5% |
| Second home for the household | 140 m² or less | 1% |
| Second home for the household | Over 140 m² | 2% |
| Third home or more | Any | 3% |
| Commercial or 商住 unit | Any | 3% |

The 140 m² line is a cliff, not a slope. A 141 m² flat is taxed as a large flat for its whole value, not just for the extra square metre. If you are choosing between two units either side of that line, the deed tax alone can differ by tens of thousands.

"First home" means first for the household — you, your spouse and your minor children, counted nationally in most cities now. A flat your spouse owns in another province may disqualify you from the 1% band.

## What only resale carries: the seller's taxes

These three exist only when the property changes hands for the second time or later. On a new build the developer has already dealt with them, and they are inside the price.

**增值税 (zēngzhíshuì, VAT).** Charged at 5% of the transaction price if the seller has held the flat for less than two years. Held for two years or more, ordinary residential property is exempt. In the largest cities, non-ordinary residential property — broadly, large or high-value units — sold after two years is charged on the gain rather than exempt entirely. The two-year clock runs from the seller's original purchase, which is why a flat bought eighteen months ago is a materially different proposition from an identical one bought five years ago.

**个人所得税 (gèrén suǒdéshuì, individual income tax).** Charged at 20% of the gain, or at 1% of the price under the assessed method most local offices default to for ordinary housing. It is exempt entirely where the flat is 满五唯一 (mǎn wǔ wéi yī, held five years and the seller's only home). A seller who owns two flats cannot claim the exemption on either, whichever one they sell.

**Land VAT and stamp duty do not apply.** Individuals selling residential property are exempt from 土地增值税 (tǔdì zēngzhíshuì, land appreciation tax), and stamp duty on residential transfers is suspended for individuals. Anyone quoting you either of these on a residential resale is working from a commercial-property template.

:::warn
VAT also drags surcharges with it — 城建税 and 教育费附加 — calculated as a percentage of the VAT itself, not of the price. They are small next to the VAT but they appear on the settlement statement and surprise people. Where VAT is exempt, they are zero.
:::

## The convention that decides who actually pays

Legally, VAT and individual income tax are the seller's obligations. In practice, most Chinese resale transactions are negotiated on a 到手价 (dàoshǒujià, "price in hand") basis: the seller names the amount they want to receive, and everything else — their taxes, sometimes both agencies' commissions — is added on top and paid by the buyer at the tax window.

This is not a trick, and it is not illegal. It is simply how the market quotes. But it means the number on the listing is not the number you pay, and the gap is not small: on a flat held under two years it can be 6% or more.

Two ways to handle it:

- **Ask for 税费各付 (each side pays its own).** Then the seller's price is genuinely the price, and you can compare it against other listings honestly. Sellers accept this more often than buyers expect, particularly when the flat has been listed for a while.
- **Ask for the seller's 原购房发票 and 契税票 (original purchase invoice and deed tax receipt).** These establish the tax basis. Without them the tax authority may assess the gain from a much lower presumed cost, and the VAT difference calculation in the largest cities becomes impossible to verify. A seller who cannot produce them is telling you something about the paperwork chain.

## What only a new build carries

A new flat from a developer has no seller-side taxes to inherit, but it has its own list, and the list is longer than most buyers budget for.

| Item | Typical amount | Notes |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 维修基金 (maintenance fund) | 2–3% of price, or a fixed rate per m² | Paid once, at handover. Held by the housing authority, not the developer |
| Property fees, prepaid | 6–12 months | Often collected at handover as a condition of getting the keys |
| 面积补差 (area settlement) | Up to ±3% of price | The surveyed area rarely matches the contract exactly. You settle at the contract rate |
| Fit-out | Highly variable | A 毛坯 (shell) unit needs a full fit-out; 精装 (finished) is included but unverifiable before handover |
| Parking | Separate title, separate price | Usually not included in the mortgage and often not in the advertised price |
| Registration and misc. | A few hundred yuan | Nominal |

The 面积补差 line is the one to read in the contract before signing. Standard practice allows settlement within 3% of the contracted area; beyond that, the buyer can generally cancel. A contract that does not state the 3% rule, or that obliges you to accept any difference at the developer's rate, is a contract written for the developer.

:::note
Utility connection fees — gas, heating, broadband — used to be a standard extra on Chinese new builds. Several cities have since forbidden developers from charging them separately. Ask whether they are included, and get the answer in the contract rather than in a sales conversation.
:::

## The line that is easy to miss: the valuation

On a resale purchase the bank lends against its own valuation, not against your agreed price. If you agree 5,000,000 and the bank values the flat at 4,600,000, your loan is calculated on 4,600,000. The 400,000 difference is cash you have to find, on top of the down payment you had already budgeted.

For foreign buyers this compounds, because loan-to-value ratios are often lower than for nationals — commonly 50–65% rather than 70%. Run the numbers on the valuation rather than the price:

- Agreed price × your assumed LTV is not the loan you will get.
- Bank valuation × your actual LTV is the loan you will get.
- The gap between the two is the number that kills deals at week six.

On a new build this problem largely disappears, because the developer's price and the bank's valuation of a new unit in the same development tend to converge. That is one honest reason new builds are popular with foreign buyers, and it has nothing to do with quality.

## A worked comparison

A 120 m² flat, first home for the household, price 5,000,000, held by the seller for three years, seller's original purchase 3,000,000.

| Line | New build | Resale |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Price | 5,000,000 | 5,000,000 |
| Deed tax (1%, under 140 m²) | 50,000 | 50,000 |
| VAT (held over 2 years) | — | 0 |
| Individual income tax (1%, not 满五唯一) | — | 50,000 |
| Maintenance fund (2%) | 100,000 | — |
| Property fees prepaid (1 year) | 7,200 | — |
| Agency commission (2%) | — | 100,000 |
| Valuation fee | — | ~1,500 |
| **Cash on completion** | **~157,000** | **~201,500** |

Now change one fact: the seller bought eighteen months ago instead of three years ago. VAT at 5% becomes payable — roughly 238,000 — and on 到手价 terms it is the buyer's cash. The resale route goes from 201,500 to about 440,000, or nearly 9% of the price.

That single date is more consequential than anything else in this comparison. It is also the first thing to establish about any resale flat you are serious about, and it is a fact, not an opinion: the seller's original purchase date is on the certificate.

## What to ask before you compare anything

:::checklist
For a resale flat:

- When did the seller buy, and is the certificate dated more than two years ago? This decides VAT.
- Is this the seller's only home? This decides individual income tax.
- Is the deal 到手价 or 税费各付? Get it in the written offer.
- Can the seller produce the original purchase invoice and deed tax receipt?
- What is the bank's valuation likely to be, and what LTV will they offer a foreign buyer?
- Is the flat 普通住宅 or 非普通住宅 by local definition? This decides whether the two-year VAT exemption is full or partial.

For a new build:

- Does the project hold a 预售许可证 (presale permit) for the specific building you are buying into?
- What is the 维修基金 rate, and is it per m² or a percentage?
- What does the contract say about 面积补差, and does it include the 3% cancellation right?
- Is 精装 included, and is there a written specification with brands and models?
- Are utility connections included in the price?
- What is the penalty clause for late delivery, and is the completion date fixed or "subject to government approvals"?
:::

## When to walk away

:::kill
Walk away in these situations, and do not let a discount talk you out of them:

- **An off-plan project without a 预售许可证 for your building.** The permit is what makes the sale legal and what puts your money into a supervised account. Without it you are lending money to a developer with no security and no priority.
- **A resale quoted on 到手价 terms where the seller refuses to disclose their purchase date and tax position.** You cannot price what you cannot see, and this is the single most common way foreign buyers overpay by 5%.
- **A resale where the seller cannot produce the original purchase documents.** The tax assessment will default against you, and the foreign-exchange paper trail for your own exit later is already broken.
- **A new build whose 精装 specification exists only in a brochure.** At handover you have no contractual basis to refuse anything.
- **Any deal where the bank valuation comes in more than about 10% below the agreed price and you cannot fund the gap in cash.** This is not a negotiation problem; it is a financing problem, and it does not improve with time.
:::

The two routes are not better or worse in the abstract. A new build trades a known fee schedule for delivery risk and an unverifiable finish. A resale trades a visible flat for a tax bill that may or may not be yours, and a valuation that may or may not support the loan. Price the risks, then choose — not the other way round.

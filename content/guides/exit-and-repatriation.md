:::answer
Selling is straightforward: documents, tax, transfer, remittance. The variable is the last step. Every renminbi of sale proceeds must be traceable to money that entered China through a compliant channel, so the decision you make on day one about how to send the purchase funds sets the ceiling on how easily you leave.
:::

Most guides to buying property in China stop at the purchase. That is like describing a boat by its hull and never mentioning the water. For a foreign buyer, the exit is not an afterthought — it is the part that determines whether the asset is worth owning at all.

Two things happen on the way out, and they are separate problems:

1. **Selling** — a legal and tax process, well-defined, with a fixed sequence.
2. **Repatriating** — a banking and documentation process, where the constraint is not the amount but the provenance of the money.

## Part one: selling

The sale itself mirrors the purchase in reverse, with one addition: you are now the seller, and the seller carries the taxes that buyers usually end up paying.

### The sequence

- **Contract and deposit** — a deposit from the buyer, held in a supervised account (资金监管, zī jīn jiān guǎn), not in your personal account.
- **Online registration** (网签) — the sale enters the housing authority's system, and the buyer's eligibility is tested there.
- **Tax assessment and payment** — at the tax window, on the registered price.
- **Transfer** (过户) — the new certificate is issued, and the escrowed balance is released to you against it.

Timeline: six to ten weeks if the buyer pays cash, eight to twelve if they borrow, plus however long it takes you to clear any mortgage of your own.

### The tax bill when you sell

The seller's side, in the current national setting (in force since the 2024 adjustment, applied in March 2026 — confirm with the local tax bureau, because municipal practice varies):

- **VAT** (增值税, zēng zhí shuì) — around 5.3% of price, generally exempt where the property has been held **two years or more**.
- **Individual income tax** (个人所得税, gè rén suǒ dé shuì) — typically 1% of price, or 20% of the gain where the original purchase documents allow the gain to be computed. Exempt where the property has been your **only home for five years or more** — 满五唯一, mǎn wǔ wéi yī.
- **Deed tax** (契税, qì shuì) — the buyer's tax, 1–3% depending on area and whether it is their first home.

The word "exempt" here is doing a lot of work, and both exemptions depend on facts you should be able to prove with documents:

- The two-year clock usually runs from the **certificate date**, not the contract date. An off-plan purchase can be five years old by contract and still under two years by certificate.
- The five-year, only-home exemption requires that you owned no other residential property in China during those five years. If you bought a second flat, the exemption is gone, and the tax on the gain can be the largest single cost of the whole investment.

This is worth saying plainly: **the tax you pay on exit can exceed several years of rent.** Run the number before you buy, not after.

### Selling from abroad

If you have left China and want to sell, you cannot simply sign from a distance. You need a notarised power of attorney (公证委托书) appointing someone in China to sign, attend the tax window and complete the transfer on your behalf.

Practical notes:

- The power of attorney is usually prepared at a Chinese embassy or consulate, or locally with legalisation or an apostille, depending on where you are. This takes weeks, and the wording must be accepted by the specific registration office — a generic document is often rejected.
- The scope matters. A power of attorney that covers "signing the contract" but not "receiving the proceeds" leaves the money stuck.
- The document has an expiry. Check the date before the transfer appointment, not after.

## Part two: getting the money out

This is the part that surprises people, and the surprise is not the tax rate — it is the provenance rule.

### The rule

Foreign currency leaving China must be matched to a legitimate source. For property sale proceeds, the bank will want to see that the original purchase money **entered China through a bank, in foreign currency, in your name, and was declared**. That record is what allows the proceeds to leave.

This has consequences that reach all the way back to the purchase:

- **Bring the money in through a bank, in your own name, with the purpose declared.** Keep the remittance advice, the bank receipt and the foreign exchange declaration (涉外收入申报单).
- **Do not bring purchase money in as cash**, and do not have a friend or relative transfer it on your behalf "to save time". A purchase funded by someone else's transfer is, from the bank's point of view, a purchase funded by someone else.
- **Do not net it off against something else.** Every renminbi should be traceable.

If the money came in badly, the property can still be sold — but the proceeds may sit in a Chinese account. That is the outcome worth avoiding, and it is decided in week one, not at the exit.

### The file the bank will ask for

Expect to produce, at the remittance bank:

1. Passport and the residence permit used at purchase, plus the record of the original inbound remittance.
2. The sale contract and the transfer record showing the property changed hands.
3. The new certificate issued to the buyer, or the registration record showing transfer.
4. Tax payment certificates — 完税证明 — for the taxes paid on the sale.
5. Proof the funds in the account came from this sale, not from something else.
6. The bank's own foreign exchange form, completed at the counter.

Banks differ in how much they ask for, and branches differ within the same bank. The branch that handled your inbound remittance is usually the most efficient place to do the outbound one.

### The US$50,000 figure

The annual foreign exchange purchase quota for an individual — US$50,000 equivalent — is a **conversion** limit for turning renminbi into foreign currency under the personal current-account rules. It is not a cap on repatriating capital you brought in.

Property proceeds belong to the capital account, where the governing principle is documentation rather than a per-person annual ceiling: money that entered compliantly may leave with the file above. In practice this means the process is slower, involves more counters and more signatures, and the bank has discretion to ask for more.

Do not plan around the idea that you can take the money out US$50,000 at a time over several years using family members' quotas. That is the arrangement most likely to be refused, and it is the one that turns a compliant exit into an investigation.

## What to do before you leave China

Five things, in the last month:

1. **Keep the bank account open.** Closing it makes the eventual repatriation harder, not easier. A dormant account with a valid passport link is worth more than a closed one.
2. **Get the tax certificates at the time of any transaction.** 完税证明 is issued at the window; asking for it two years later is a different, slower process.
3. **Scan everything.** Certificate, contracts, receipts, remittance advices, tax certificates — as PDFs, in a place you can reach from outside China.
4. **Register the power of attorney now, if you plan to sell from abroad.** Not when you need it.
5. **Check your residence registration is closed properly** if you are leaving, and that no utility or management account is left in your name. Unpaid property management fees follow the property and complicate a future sale.

:::warn
If you are buying with money that has already been inside China — an inheritance, salary savings, a sale of another asset — the provenance rule still applies, but the documentation is different: the record of how that money was earned and taxed. Ask the bank, before you buy, what they would need to see to send the proceeds of a future sale abroad. A thirty-minute conversation with the remittance desk is the cheapest insurance in the whole process.
:::

:::kill
Walk away from a purchase where the funds would have to enter as cash, through a third party's account, or as an undeclared transfer. The property may be excellent. The exit will be the problem, and the problem will not appear until it is too late to fix.
:::

:::checklist
Inbound remittance advices kept · foreign exchange declaration kept · purpose stated at the time of transfer · tax certificates collected at each transaction · bank account kept open after leaving · power of attorney prepared before it is needed · all documents scanned and stored off-device · remittance desk asked in advance what the exit file requires · exit tax computed before the purchase, not after.
:::

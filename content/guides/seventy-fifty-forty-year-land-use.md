:::answer
You never own land in China. You own a building plus a use-right over state land, for 70, 50 or 40 years — counted from the grant date, which is earlier than you think.
:::

This is the single most misunderstood feature of Chinese property among foreign buyers, and it is misunderstood in both directions: some treat the term as irrelevant, others treat it as a time bomb. Neither is right.

## The three terms

| Land use | Term | What sits on it |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Residential (住宅) | 70 years | Apartments, houses, villa compounds |
| Industrial, education, science, culture, health, sports | 50 years | Factories, campuses, hospitals, some mixed-use |
| Commercial, tourism, entertainment | 40 years | Offices, shops, hotels, "loft" apartments |

The term attaches to the land parcel, not to you. When you buy a resale unit you inherit the remainder of the term, not a fresh 70 years.

## The clock starts earlier than you think

Land is granted to the developer years before the building is finished. A project granted land in 2012, built in 2015 and sold to you in 2026 has roughly **56 years remaining**, not 70. A 2003 grant with a 2026 purchase has 47.

This matters for two practical reasons:

**Your mortgage term.** Banks cap the loan term by the remaining land term, not just by your age. A unit with 30 years left will not support a 30-year mortgage.

**Your exit.** Buyers increasingly ask. A 20-year old building with 50 years remaining is a normal purchase; a 40-year-old building with 30 years remaining is a discount conversation.

:::note
Ask for the land grant date (土地出让年限起始日) in writing. It appears on the title certificate and on the developer's land grant contract. Agents often quote the build date instead, which flatters the remaining term by three to five years.
:::

## What happens at expiry

**Residential (70 years).** The Civil Code provides that residential land-use terms renew automatically (自动续期). The mechanism is settled in principle; the fee, if any, is left to future legislation. Nobody has yet had a residential term expire and be refused renewal, because the earliest 70-year grants date from the late 1980s and early 1990s.

**Non-residential (40 and 50 years).** Renewal requires an application and may be granted with a land premium payable, or refused. This is a materially weaker position, and it is the reason the 40-year market prices at a discount.

**Buildings on the land.** The building itself remains yours; what expires is the right to use the land beneath it. In practice, renewal and redevelopment are handled together.

## Why 40-year units are a different asset

"Loft" apartments sold as 商住 or 酒店式公寓 are commercial-use units dressed as residences. They are cheaper per square metre, and the discount is not a bargain — it reflects a different set of rules:

| Feature | 70-year residential | 40-year 商住 |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Buying as a foreign individual | Permitted, one unit | Generally not permitted |
| Utility rates | Residential | Commercial, often 2–3× |
| School catchment and household registration | Yes | No |
| Mortgage | Up to 25–30 years, 50–60% LTV | Shorter term, often 50% LTV |
| Deed tax | 1–2% | Typically 3% |
| Resale taxes | Standard residential | Higher, with VAT often payable |
| Term renewal | Automatic in principle | Application, possible premium |

For a foreign buyer the eligibility row is usually decisive: these units are closed to foreign individuals in most cities. If someone offers you one, that is the moment to verify rather than negotiate.

## The checklist before you commit

:::checklist
- Land grant date, and the remaining term calculated from it
- Land use category on the title certificate — a single character changes the asset class
- Remaining term against your intended holding period and mortgage term
- For anything non-residential: written confirmation of foreign-buyer eligibility
- Utility tariff confirmation in writing, from the utility, not the agent
- Any heritage, planning or demolition order affecting the parcel
:::

## The honest summary

For a 70-year residential unit, the term is a slow decay, not a cliff, and the renewal framework is favourable. For a 40-year unit, the term is a real constraint that shows up in taxes, financing, utilities and eligibility — and the low price is the market telling you so.

:::answer
Four payments, in order: a small deposit against a written agreement, the down payment into a supervised escrow account, the balance at registration, and the retention at handover. The seller should never hold your money before the title has moved.
:::

Foreign buyers lose money in Chinese property transactions in a small number of predictable ways, and paying the seller directly is the most common. The mechanism that prevents it already exists, is widely used domestically, and is routinely skipped by buyers who do not know to ask for it.

## The four payments

| Stage | Chinese term | Typical size | Goes to | Trigger to release |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Deposit | 定金 | 1–5% of price | Seller or escrow | Signed deposit agreement, title search clean |
| Down payment | 首付 | 30–70% | Supervised escrow | Contract registered (网签), eligibility confirmed |
| Balance | 尾款 | Remainder | Supervised escrow | Ownership transfer completed |
| Retention | 交房尾款 | 1–5% | Escrow | Handover inspection passed, utilities settled |

## What the supervised account actually is

A 资金监管 account is opened in your name at a bank, under a tripartite arrangement involving the bank, the buyer and the seller — and in most cities the housing authority supervises it as part of the registration process.

The money sits there, not with the seller, until a defined event occurs. The bank will not release it on the seller's instruction alone, and it will not release it to you unilaterally either. The release conditions are written into the supervision agreement, and they mirror the registration steps.

This is why the account is worth the inconvenience: it converts a trust problem into a document problem.

## The clauses that decide everything

:::checklist
- **Release conditions**, stated as documents: contract registration number, tax receipt, new title certificate
- **Deposit refund trigger**: title defects, failed eligibility, declined financing — each with a defined deadline
- **Seller breach penalty**: double the deposit, payable from escrow rather than by negotiation
- **Date certain for registration**, with a consequence if the seller stalls
- **Vacant possession date**, and the cost of staying past it
- **Outstanding fees cap**: management fees, utilities and property tax settled by the seller or deducted
- **Fixtures and fittings schedule**, with photographs, before handover
:::

:::warn
A deposit paid directly to a seller's personal account is, in practical terms, an unsecured loan to a stranger. If the seller disappears, is subject to a court seal, or simply changes their mind, your remedy is litigation in a Chinese court, in Chinese, over a period measured in years. The escrow account exists precisely so that you never need that remedy.
:::

## The sequence, week by week

**Week 1 — offer accepted.** Sign the deposit agreement. It should contain the release logic, not just the amount. Pay the deposit into escrow if the seller will agree, which they usually will once a competent agent is involved.

**Week 2 — due diligence.** Title search at the district registry, seller identity and authority check, planning and land-term check, outstanding fee check. Do this before the down payment, not after.

**Week 3 — contract registration (网签).** The sale is entered into the housing authority's system. This is the moment your eligibility is formally tested and the property is locked against a second sale.

**Week 4 — tax assessment and payment.** Deed tax and any seller-side taxes are assessed. Payment produces receipts, which are a release condition.

**Week 5–7 — transfer and balance.** Ownership transfers, the new certificate is issued, and escrow releases the balance.

**Week 8 — handover.** Final inspection, meter readings, management fee settlement, keys. Retain the last slice until this is done.

## Where foreign buyers get stuck

**The eligibility check lands after the deposit.** If your one-year record turns out to be short, you need the deposit back, and you need the agreement to say so. Otherwise you are negotiating with a seller who has your money and no incentive.

**The funds arrive late.** Escrow release requires the money to be there. A remittance that clears two weeks late can breach a defined date and trigger a penalty. Build a two-week buffer into every date.

**The seller still lives there.** Vacant possession is a clause, not an assumption. Write down the date, the penalty, and who pays the management fees in between.

**Someone suggests paying "off-book" to reduce tax.** A lower registered price reduces tax and also reduces what you can repatriate later, and it creates an inconsistent file that a bank will eventually find. Do not.

## The one-page rule

If you remember nothing else: **money moves against documents, never against promises.** Every payment should be released by a specific document being produced, and the documents should be listed in the agreement before the first yuan moves.

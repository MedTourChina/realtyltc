:::answer
Four searches clear most of the risk: the title registry, the encumbrance file, the court seal record, and the building's planning and heritage status. None of them takes more than a few days, and all of them can be done by a lawyer with a power of attorney before you pay anything.
:::

Due diligence in a Chinese resale purchase is not a survey and a structural report, the way it might be in your home country. The building is usually recent enough that structural failure is not the risk. The risk is legal: who actually owns this, what is registered against it, and whether the thing the seller is describing is the thing on the certificate.

Work through this in order. Each section tells you what the check rules out, because a checklist you do not understand is a checklist you will skip.

## The four searches

### 1. The title registry

The property ownership certificate — 不动产权证, bù dòng chǎn quán zhèng — is the single document that records who owns the building and the land-use right beneath it. It replaced the older pair of 房产证 and 土地证.

A registry search (不动产登记资料查询) at the district registration centre, with the seller's written consent, returns the certificate's content and its history. Read five fields:

- **Nature of right** — 权利性质. Should be 出让 (granted) for a normal purchase. 划拨 (allocated) land is a different animal and can require a payment to convert.
- **Use** — 用途. Must be residential (住宅) if you are buying a home. Anything else changes the tax, the mortgage, and in many cities your eligibility.
- **Term end date** — the land-use right expires 70 years from the grant for residential. A short remaining term is a price issue, not just a paperwork issue.
- **Registered area** — 建筑面积, which includes shared area (公摊). Compare it to what you were shown.
- **Owner names** — all of them. If there are two and only one is signing, stop.

### 2. The encumbrances

The same search shows registered charges: mortgages (抵押, dǐ yā), and anything else registered against the property. An existing mortgage must be discharged — 抵押注销 — before transfer. The discharge takes two to six weeks and needs the seller's money, not yours.

The trap is the promise. "The bank will release it" is not a discharge. Ask for the discharge certificate, or for a written schedule with a date, before the deposit.

### 3. Court seals

A seal — 查封, chá fēng — is a legal freeze, usually from litigation or a debt. A sealed property cannot transfer at all. This is the check that saves buyers from paying a deposit on something that cannot legally be sold, and it is the reason a registry search is not optional.

Also ask whether there is a pending lawsuit involving the seller. A seal can be applied after your search and before your transfer.

### 4. Planning, heritage and demolition

At the district planning office, or on the published planning notice (规划公示), check whether the building or the block carries:

- A heritage protection order (历史建筑保护) — which restricts alteration and can restrict sale.
- A demolition or expropriation notice (征收决定).
- A change of use application.
- An unresolved building-code issue from construction.

A demolition notice does not always show up in the price, because the seller may know and you may not.

## The nine documents

Ask for these as a set. If any is missing, ask why in writing.

1. The property ownership certificate, original, and a scan.
2. The seller's ID, and the marriage certificate if married.
3. The spouse's written consent to sell, or their presence at signing.
4. The original purchase contract and the deed tax receipt from when the seller bought.
5. The mortgage discharge certificate, or the loan balance statement.
6. The property management fee settlement, showing no arrears.
7. The maintenance fund (维修基金) payment receipt.
8. The building's completion acceptance certificate (竣工验收备案), for newer buildings.
9. For an off-plan unit: the pre-sale permit (预售许可证) covering your specific building.

Item 4 matters more than it looks. The seller's original purchase price is what the tax authority uses to compute the gain, and a missing receipt can turn a small tax bill into a large one.

## The spouse question

In Chinese practice, a residential property bought during a marriage is treated as joint property even when only one name is on the certificate. That means the spouse's consent is required, and the registration office will ask for it.

If the spouse is abroad, the consent needs a notarised power of attorney (公证委托书), and in many cases legalisation or an apostille at the Chinese embassy or consulate. This takes weeks. It is one of the five standard reasons a transaction stalls.

Ask early, in the first week: "Is the seller married, and where is the spouse?"

## The four physical inspections

Documentary checks tell you what is registered. These tell you what you are actually buying.

**1. The unit, at two different times of day.** Morning and evening. Light, noise, and who is in the corridor.

**2. The building's systems.** Lifts (age, count, service record), water pressure on the top floor, heating if the city has it, and the state of the corridor walls — a corridor that has not been painted in a decade tells you what the management fee has been spent on.

**3. The management office.** Four questions: the fee per square metre, arrears in this unit, whether the maintenance fund has been drawn down, and how many units in the building are rented out rather than owner-occupied.

**4. The neighbours.** One conversation at the door is worth an hour with the agent. Ask how long they have lived there and whether the building has any ongoing dispute — over the lifts, the parking, or a planned change of use.

## Reading the certificate like a professional

Three patterns are worth memorising.

**Use is commercial but the seller calls it an apartment.** A 40- or 50-year unit sold as living space is commercial-residential stock — 商住, shāng zhù. Commercial utilities, no school catchment, 3% deed tax, shorter mortgages, and in most cities foreign individual buyers cannot register it at all. The price difference is not a bargain; it is a different asset.

**Area sits just under 140m².** The deed tax steps at 140m². A 139m² and a 141m² unit are not the same purchase, and a seller splitting a unit to land under the line is creating a registration problem, not a tax saving.

**Two names, one signature.** Already covered, and it is the most common way a deal dies at the counter.

:::warn
A registry search performed on Monday does not cover Tuesday. If the seller has creditors, ask for a second search in the week of transfer, and make the contract say that a seal discovered before transfer cancels the sale and returns the deposit.
:::

:::kill
Walk away if: the registry search cannot be obtained at all; there is a seal on the property; the seller will not produce a mortgage discharge schedule; the use is not residential and you intend to live in it; the spouse will not consent; the building carries a demolition notice; or the seller asks you to register a price below what you are paying.
:::

:::checklist
Registry search done and read in full · encumbrances listed and a discharge date agreed in writing · seal check done within the last week · planning and heritage status checked · nine documents collected · spouse's location and consent confirmed · management office asked about arrears, fees and rental ratio · building systems inspected · contract names a second seal check before transfer · deposit sits in a supervised account.
:::

:::answer
Judge a city on seven dimensions, and judge three of them harder than the rest: the size of the foreign buyer pool (your exit), the depth of English-language professional services (your error correction), and the school and commute reality (your daily life). Price is the fourth question, not the first.
:::

Most city comparisons are price tables. A price table tells you where property is cheap, which is not the same as where you should buy — and in a market where exit takes quarters rather than weeks, the second question matters more.

## The seven dimensions

Every city on this site is assessed on the same set, which is what makes the comparison honest rather than a set of unrelated impressions.

| # | Dimension | What you are judging |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | Eligibility practice | How strictly the national framework is applied locally |
| 2 | Price band | Median secondary asking price per m², with a date |
| 3 | Gross yield | Annual rent over price, before costs and vacancy |
| 4 | Foreign buyer pool | How many people could plausibly buy from you later |
| 5 | Service depth | English-speaking counsel, notaries, banks that have done foreign files |
| 6 | Schools and commute | International schools, and the commute to your actual workplace |
| 7 | Market-specific risk | The thing that goes wrong in this city and nowhere else |

## The three that decide

**The buyer pool.** Your future buyer is a narrow group: another foreign national with the same permit constraints, or a Chinese national who wants that building. In Shanghai and Beijing that group is large enough to produce a transaction within a few months. In Wuhan or Qingdao it may take a year, and the discount required to move it is not small. This is not a reason to avoid smaller markets; it is a reason to price the illiquidity in.

**Service depth.** When something goes wrong at registration — and something usually does — you need a lawyer who has seen the specific problem in that city, a notary who accepts your documents, and a bank that has converted funds for a property purchase before. Cities with thin service depth convert a two-week fix into a three-month negotiation. That cost almost never appears in a price comparison.

**School and commute.** If you have children, the school decision constrains the district, and the district constrains everything else. Families routinely buy a good apartment in the wrong district and then spend two years commuting to a school they could have lived next to.

:::rule
Choose the district by the school and the commute. Choose the building inside that district by the management, the light and the land term. Choose the unit last. Most people do it in exactly the opposite order.
:::

## How to read a city in an afternoon

You do not need a site visit to rule a city out, and a site visit will not tell you what you need unless you know what you are looking for.

:::checklist
- Pull the municipal housing bureau's monthly transaction disclosures for the last twelve months — headlines lie, disclosures do not
- Ask an agent to print registered transaction prices for one building you are considering, not listing prices
- Check the rent actually achieved on comparable units, and count the weeks of vacancy in the listings
- Map three international schools against your workplace, at 8am, on the metro
- Find out whether a lawyer in that city has handled a foreign purchase file in the last year — if not, that is your answer about service depth
- Ask what happens to the buyer pool if the largest employer in that district leaves
:::

## The comparison trap

Two cities can look equivalent in a price table and be completely different in practice. A ¥30,000/m² market with a deep buyer pool and good counsel is a safer purchase than a ¥22,000/m² market with neither, even though the second looks like a bargain.

The trap is comparing what you can measure (price per square metre) while ignoring what you cannot (how long it takes to sell, who will help you when it breaks).

## City is downstream of motive

A city judgement without a horizon is meaningless. If you are staying seven years, the school matters and the buyer pool matters less. If you are staying three years, the opposite is true, and the honest advice is not to buy at all.

This is why layer 2 comes first. If you have not written down a horizon, go back and do it — the city analysis will keep changing until you do.

## When to stop

:::checklist
- The city has fewer than a handful of recent foreign-buyer transactions — stop and reconsider
- You cannot name the district you would live in — stop, you are not ready to buy
- The school placement is unresolved — stop, the district decision depends on it
- You would be buying in a district you have never visited at 8am on a weekday — stop
- Your employer's registered address is elsewhere — stop, this is layer 1 again
:::

:::answer
Pick the district on four measurable things — commute, school, services, resale pool — not on how the street feels on a Saturday afternoon. The building is the last decision you make, because it is the only one you can change later.
:::

Most buyers do this in the wrong order. They see a flat they like, then ask whether the area is good. By then the area has been chosen for them by a listing photograph, and every subsequent question is asked in the shadow of a decision already made.

A district is the only part of a purchase you cannot renovate. You can replace a kitchen, add insulation, redo a bathroom. You cannot move the compound two metro stops closer to your office, and you cannot move a school catchment. So the district comes first, and it is decided on four things, three of which can be tested in a day.

## One: the commute, measured honestly

Not the distance on a map, and not what the agent says. Door to door, on a weekday, at the hour you will actually travel.

Stand at the compound gate at 07:45 and travel to your workplace. Count the minutes from the moment you leave the building to the moment you sit down, including the walk to the station, the wait, the transfer, and the walk at the other end. Do it once in the morning and once at 18:30.

The number you get is usually 1.4 to 1.8 times what the map suggests, because maps measure the straight line between two points and cities are not straight lines. If the measured figure is more than about fifty minutes each way, the property is competing against every other property you will see, and it will lose on resale to something closer.

There is a second reason to measure it: it tells you who else lives there. A compound where most residents leave at 07:30 by metro is a compound of working households, and working households sell to working households. A compound that is empty at 09:00 and full at 21:00 is something else, and it usually resells more slowly.

## Two: the school question

If you have children, this is the deciding filter and everything else is secondary. The catchment — 学区, xué qū — is an administrative mapping from address to school place, and it is repriced by policy rather than by the market. A compound can lose its place in a single announcement.

Three practical rules:

- Confirm the current catchment with the district education bureau (教育局), not with the agent, not with the developer's brochure, and not with a neighbour. Ask for the mapping in writing, and ask when it was last revised.
- Ask how many places are allocated to the compound this year, and how many households applied. A catchment with more applicants than places is a lottery, and a lottery is not a guarantee.
- If the property is new, ask whether the school has opened. A catchment for a school that does not exist yet is a planning intention, not a school place.

If you do not have children, the school question still matters, because it is priced into the unit. You are buying the premium and the policy risk at the same time. In districts with strong catchments, a meaningful part of the asking price is the school, and that part can be removed by an administrative decision that has nothing to do with the building.

## Three: services within walking distance

This is the filter that foreign buyers consistently undervalue when they are new, and consistently overvalue once they have lived here for a year. The question is not whether the area is "international". The question is what you can reach in fifteen minutes on foot or by one direct bus.

Walk the following route and note what you pass:

- A hospital or clinic where a doctor will see you without a Chinese-speaking friend. Not necessarily an international clinic, but somewhere with a registration process you can navigate.
- A supermarket that stocks what you actually cook with. This is a smaller problem than it was ten years ago, but it still separates districts.
- Public transport that goes where you need to go directly, rather than requiring two transfers.
- A bank branch that has handled a foreign passport recently. Foreign-exchange and remittance business is branch-specific in practice; a branch that has never done it will take three visits.

The reason this is a filter rather than a preference: the same set of services is what makes the unit sellable to the next foreign or returnee buyer. A district where you can live without a car is a district with a wider resale pool.

## Four: the resale pool

Before you look at any unit, ask who will buy it from you, and how long that will take.

Look at how many comparable units in the same compound are currently listed, and how long they have been listed. A compound with forty identical units on the market is a compound where you are not selling a home, you are joining a queue. A compound with one or two listings is usually a compound people do not leave, which is a different risk: you may have to wait to buy in, and wait to sell out.

Ask the property management office two questions: how many units are owner-occupied, and how many are rented. A compound that is mostly rented has a different wear pattern, a different set of neighbours, and a thinner owner resale market. A compound that is entirely owner-occupied and elderly is a compound where the next buyer will be a family, which is fine, but the timing of your sale will be dictated by their financing.

## What a district visit should actually look like

One day, six stops, and no viewing appointments until the day is done.

1. **07:45, the gate.** Do the commute. Note how many people leave at the same time, and how they leave.
2. **10:00, the property management office.** Ask about management fee arrears in the building, the fee per square metre, whether the lifts have been replaced, and how many units are rented out.
3. **12:00, lunch.** Eat where residents eat. Note whether the restaurants are chains, canteens, or nothing at all.
4. **15:00, the school gate.** Even without children. Watch the dismissal. Is it a school with a queue of cars and grandparents, or a school with a wall and no one at the gate?
5. **18:30, the same commute in reverse.** This is the number that matters most.
6. **20:00, the compound at night.** Lighting, noise, parking, and whether anyone is outside.

Only after those six stops should you view a unit. If the district fails at 07:45 or 18:30, the flat does not matter.

## Once the district is settled: the compound

Then, and only then, the building questions:

- **Management fee** — 物业费, wù yè fèi — per square metre per month. On a 120m² flat at ¥8, that is ¥11,520 a year, which is a real number against a rent of ¥5,000–7,000 a month in most second-tier cities.
- **Shared area** — 公摊, gōng tān — the part of the registered area that is corridors, lifts and common space, commonly 20–30%. It is why a 100m² unit can feel like 75m².
- **Lift age and count.** One lift for twelve floors is a daily negotiation. A lift past fifteen years is a special assessment waiting to happen.
- **Parking ratio.** Below about 0.8 spaces per unit, parking becomes a separate market with its own price.
- **Mixed use.** A building with offices or short-lets on the same floors has a different fire standard, a different neighbour pattern, and a different resale buyer.

## Streets that look good and fail

- **The new district with no occupancy.** Empty at 20:00, no shops open, and every unit identical. The price is attractive because there is no secondary market yet, and there may not be one when you sell.
- **The old central district with character.** No lift, no parking, and a management fee that does not cover the roof. You are buying the location and inheriting the building's maintenance debt.
- **Anything within a few hundred metres of a demolition or heritage order.** Check the planning notice board (规划公示) at the district office. An order does not always reduce the price, because the seller knows and you may not.
- **A compound where a third of the units are short-let.** The building's wear, security and neighbour relations all change, and owner-occupier buyers avoid it.

:::warn
Do not let a district's reputation substitute for the visit. Districts are large. A compound on the good side of a ring road and a compound on the other side can share a name and share nothing else. Judge the compound, using the district as a filter.
:::

:::kill
Walk away when any of these is true: the measured commute exceeds your stated limit on two separate days; the school place cannot be confirmed in writing and you have children; the compound has more than about 30% of units on short-let or vacant; or the only answer to "who will buy this from me" is "another foreigner like me".
:::

:::checklist
Before you view a second property: measured the commute at 07:45 and 18:30 · confirmed the school catchment in writing, or confirmed you do not need it · walked the fifteen-minute service radius · counted comparable listings in the compound and asked how long they have been listed · asked the management office for the fee, arrears and rental ratio · read the district planning notice board · written down the one thing that would make you walk away.
:::

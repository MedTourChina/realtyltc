:::answer
Yes, but from fewer banks and on stricter terms: expect 50–60% loan-to-value, a term capped by your age, and income that a Chinese bank can verify from Chinese records.
:::

The loan is rarely the hard part. The hard part is proving income in a form a Chinese credit officer accepts, and sequencing the approval so that a decline does not arrive after you have signed a contract with a penalty attached.

## Who will actually lend

| Lender type | Appetite for foreign borrowers | Comment |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Large state-owned commercial banks | Moderate | The most common route; branch-level discretion matters |
| Joint-stock commercial banks | Moderate | Often more flexible on documentation, tighter on rate |
| City commercial banks | Variable | Sometimes the only option in tier-two cities |
| Foreign bank subsidiaries | Low | Individual residential mortgages have largely withdrawn |
| Non-bank lenders | Avoid | Cost and structure usually work against you |

Branch-level variation is real. Two branches of the same bank in the same city can give different answers, because the credit officer, not the head office, decides whether your file is workable. It is worth asking two or three.

## The terms you should expect

**Loan-to-value: 50–60%.** Some banks will stretch to 70% for a private-banking client with a deposit relationship. Below 50% is unusual; above 70% should be treated as a claim to verify.

**Term: up to 25–30 years, capped by age.** A common formulation is that the loan must be repaid by age 65–70. A buyer at 55 is typically looking at a 10–15 year term, which changes the monthly payment more than the interest rate does.

**Rate: benchmark-linked.** Pricing follows the loan prime rate plus or minus a spread, repriced annually. First-home pricing has been at or slightly below the five-year benchmark in most cities. Fixating on a tenth of a percentage point is less useful than checking the repricing terms.

**Fees:** valuation, arrangement, and sometimes an insurance requirement. Ask for a total-cost figure, not the headline rate.

## What the bank will ask you for

:::checklist
- Passport and a work-type residence permit with sufficient remaining validity
- Foreigner work permit and employment contract
- Two years of individual income tax records, or a payroll history the bank can verify
- Employer letter confirming position, salary and that the contract extends beyond the loan application
- Bank statements for the down payment, showing a documented source
- Existing property declaration — a second property changes everything
- Marriage certificate, and the spouse's consent to the mortgage if applicable
:::

:::warn
The single most common failure is income that is real but not verifiable in China. If your salary is paid offshore, or split between an onshore and offshore entity, prepare a documented explanation before applying. A credit officer who cannot map your income to a Chinese record will decline, politely, and late.
:::

## The sequence that protects you

1. **Pre-approval first.** Get an indicative decision from two banks before you make an offer. Not a promise — a written indication of loan amount, term and rate.
2. **Offer with a financing condition.** Put a clause in the deposit agreement that the deposit is refundable if financing is declined within a defined period. Sellers resist this; it is worth paying a little more for.
3. **Valuation.** The bank values the property independently of the price you agreed. A low valuation reduces your loan even if you overpaid.
4. **Approval and mortgage registration.** The mortgage is registered against the property; disbursement follows registration, not signature.
5. **Disbursement to escrow.** The bank pays into the supervised account, not to the seller directly.

## Three things buyers get wrong

**Assuming the loan follows the price.** It follows the valuation. If you agree ¥10M and the bank values at ¥9M, a 60% loan becomes ¥5.4M, not ¥6M. Your cash requirement rises by ¥600,000 at exactly the moment you can least renegotiate.

**Ignoring the early-repayment terms.** Chinese mortgages often restrict prepayment in the first one to three years, or charge a fee. If your plan involves selling within three years, read that clause before you sign, not after.

**Treating the rate as the decision.** A 0.2% rate difference on a ¥5M loan over 15 years is real money, but a one-year-shorter term or a ¥300,000 lower valuation is larger. Compare total cost, not headline rate.

## If you cannot get a mortgage

The honest alternatives, in order:

- **Buy smaller.** A 90m² unit at 1% deed tax and a lower price solves the problem more cleanly than an exotic structure.
- **Borrow against assets outside China.** Cheaper and faster than a Chinese mortgage for many foreign buyers, subject to your home-country tax position.
- **Rent.** At gross yields of 1.5–2% in the major cities, renting is not the financially irrational choice it is often presented as.

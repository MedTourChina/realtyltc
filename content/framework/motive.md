:::answer
Run the arithmetic before the emotion. In the major cities gross yields run 1.4–2%, round-trip transaction costs run 8–11%, and a holding period under five years usually costs more than it returns. If you expect to leave within three years, rent.
:::

Layer 1 asked whether you may buy. This layer asks whether you should, and it is the one people skip — because it is the only layer where the answer is "no" for reasons that feel like caution rather than fact.

## The arithmetic nobody runs

Four numbers decide most cases.

**Gross yield.** Annual rent divided by purchase price. In Shanghai, Beijing and Shenzhen it sits around 1.4–1.9%. In tier-two cities it reaches 2.0–3.4%. Compare that with your mortgage rate, currently in the low 3s for a first home, and the gap is not small.

**Round-trip cost.** Deed tax, commission, notarisation on the way in; VAT, income tax and commission on the way out. Call it 8–11% depending on how long you hold and whether the seller's taxes migrate to you.

**Holding period.** A 9% round trip over three years is 3% a year before you have paid a single management fee. Over ten years it is 0.9% a year. The same cost, divided by a different number of years.

**The alternative.** What else would the money be doing? If it would sit in a deposit account at 1.5%, the comparison is generous to property. If it would be in a global equity portfolio, it is not.

## Four questions that settle it

:::checklist
1. How many years will I actually live in this city? Write a number, not a feeling.
2. If I had to sell in year three, what would the round trip cost me?
3. If I rented an equivalent home instead, what would my net worth be in year five?
4. Am I buying a home, or buying relief from the fear of missing out?
:::

Question 3 is the one to actually calculate, and the [rent versus buy tool](/tools/rent-vs-buy/) exists for it. Question 4 is the one people answer dishonestly.

## The table

| Your situation | Position | Why |
| --- | --- | --- |
| In China ≤ 3 years | Rent | Round-trip cost exceeds plausible gain |
| 4–6 years, certain to occupy | Buy is defensible | Treat as consumption with a residual value |
| 7+ years, family stable | Buy is reasonable | Costs amortise; use value is real |
| Primary goal is appreciation | Don't | The yield arithmetic does not support it |
| You need liquidity within a year | Don't | The buyer pool is small and slow |
| You would buy a unit you would not live in | Don't | You are speculating with a residential permit |

:::rule
A home you will live in for a decade is a legitimate purchase even at a 1.6% yield. A home you will live in for eighteen months is not a purchase at all — it is a very expensive rental with a large deposit.
:::

## What "buying as consumption" means in practice

Once you accept the apartment is not an investment, the criteria change in useful ways:

- **Building quality and management matter more than appreciation.** You will live with the lifts, the insulation and the property manager.
- **Layout beats location premium at the margin.** A well-planned 110m² in a decent district usually serves a family better than a cramped 95m² in a fashionable one.
- **Renovation cost becomes part of the purchase price.** Budget ¥3,000–8,000/m² for anything older than fifteen years.
- **Exit still matters, just less.** You may sell in ten years, and the buyer then will care about the land term, the building age and the management — the same things you should care about now.

## The three numbers people get wrong

**They use asking rent instead of achieved rent.** A unit advertised at ¥14,000 a month may transact at ¥11,500 after two months vacant. Two months of vacancy on a 1.7% gross yield takes it to 1.4%.

**They forget the recurring costs.** Management fee at ¥8/m² on 120m² is ¥11,520 a year. Heating in the north adds another ¥3,000. On ¥220,000 of annual rent, that is a seventh of the income before any vacancy.

**They assume the mortgage is the cost of the money.** A 3.2% mortgage on a 1.6% yielding asset has a negative carry of 1.6% a year on the borrowed amount, before principal. That is not an argument against buying a home; it is an argument against calling it an investment.

## When to stop

:::checklist
- Your horizon is under five years — stop
- You cannot describe the exit buyer — stop
- The yield is under 1.5% and you are borrowing — stop
- You are buying because a colleague did — stop
- You would not live in the unit yourself — stop
- You cannot afford a 10% price decline without changing your plans — stop
:::

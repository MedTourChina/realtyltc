:::answer
Resale if you need certainty; off-plan if you can wait two years and can afford to be wrong about the finish.
:::

The two markets are priced against each other, and the gap between them is a fairly honest measure of what the market thinks delivery risk is worth. For a foreign buyer with limited local recourse, that gap usually understates the risk.

## The structural differences

| | Off-plan (新房) | Resale (二手房) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| What you buy | A unit in a building that does not yet exist | A registered, existing unit |
| Payment | Staged over 12–24 months | Lump sum at registration, or mortgage |
| Price per m² | Lower, particularly at first release | Higher, and visible |
| Delivery | 2–3 years, sometimes more | Immediate on handover |
| What you can inspect | A show flat, a model, and a rendering | The actual unit, building and neighbours |
| Title position | Clear at delivery, if the developer performs | Clear now, or the deal stops |
| Negotiation | Fixed schedule, little room | Real, and worth doing |
| Resale liquidity on exit | Constrained by the certificate date | Available immediately |

## The pre-sale permit is the gate

A developer may only sell before completion if the project holds a valid pre-sale permit (预售许可证). The permit is property-specific and stage-specific. If a project is being sold without one, or the permit covers a different building, the payment you make is not protected in the way it is supposed to be.

:::warn
Verify the permit number against the project, the building and the unit you are buying. "The project has a permit" is not the same statement, and the difference is the difference between a recoverable payment and an unsecured claim against a developer.
:::

Payments for pre-sale units must go into a supervised account (监管账户) tied to that specific project. Any request to pay into a developer's general account, an affiliated company, or a "marketing" account is a red flag, regardless of how normal it is presented as being.

## What actually goes wrong with off-plan

**Delivery slippage.** Two years becomes three. The contract usually provides for a penalty, which is usually a small percentage, which is usually not worth litigating over.

**Specification drift.** The show flat is a sales instrument. Flooring, kitchen brands, ceiling height and window glazing can all move, legally, within the tolerance of the contract. Read the specification annex, and understand that "or equivalent" is doing a lot of work.

**Area difference.** The completed unit's registered area can differ slightly from the contracted area. Contracts normally provide for a price adjustment within a band; outside the band, you may have a termination right that is worth knowing about.

**The certificate delay.** You cannot sell until the ownership certificate is issued, which happens after completion and can lag by a year or more. Your two-year horizon is really three.

**Developer distress.** The reason the discount exists. Supervised accounts and pre-sale permits reduce the risk; they do not eliminate it. Check the developer's delivery record on other projects before you rely on their marketing.

## Why resale suits foreign buyers better, usually

Three reasons:

1. **You can inspect the thing you are buying**, including the building management, the neighbours and the actual light at 4pm.
2. **The title is checkable now.** A resale either has a clean certificate or it does not, and you find out in week two rather than year three.
3. **You can exit.** If your assignment ends, a resale unit can be sold as soon as you own it. An off-plan unit cannot.

The counter-argument is honest too: new stock has better layouts, lifts, insulation and management, and the price per square metre is genuinely lower. If you are staying for a decade, the new building is often the better home.

## If you do buy off-plan

:::checklist
- Verify the pre-sale permit against the specific building and unit
- Confirm the supervised account number in writing, and pay only into it
- Read the specification annex line by line, and photograph the show flat
- Check the developer's record on two previously delivered projects
- Understand the area-adjustment band and your termination right
- Confirm the certificate timeline, not just the handover date
- Ask what happens to your payments if the project is delayed beyond contract
:::

## The decision rule

:::note
If your horizon is under five years, buy resale. If it is over ten and you can absorb a two-year delay, a new building is often better value. Between five and ten, the answer depends on the specific developer, and the discount should be large enough to compensate for the risk you are taking.
:::

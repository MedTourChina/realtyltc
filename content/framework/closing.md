Supervised escrow, documents as release conditions, and a written refund trigger for the deposit. If a seller wants cash before the title search, or wants to understate the price, the transaction is not worth rescuing — those two requests are the signature of every deal that ends badly.

By the time you reach this layer you have established that you may buy, decided that you should, chosen a city, and picked an asset class. All that remains is execution — which is where foreign buyers lose money, not because the rules are unknown but because the pressure at this point is entirely one-directional.

## Money moves against documents

The principle is simple and worth stating as a rule: **every payment is released by a specific document being produced.** Not by a promise, not by a relationship, not by an agent's assurance that this is how it is always done.

| Payment | Released by |
| --- | --- |
| Deposit | Clean title search plus a signed deposit agreement with a refund trigger |
| Down payment | Contract registration (网签) and confirmed eligibility |
| Balance | Transfer of ownership and the new certificate |
| Retention | Handover inspection, meter readings, fee settlement |

The mechanism that makes this work is the supervised account (资金监管, zī jīn jiān guǎn) — opened in your name, controlled jointly, and released only on the conditions written into the supervision agreement. It is standard in domestic transactions. It is routinely skipped by buyers who do not know to ask.

:::warn
A deposit paid into a seller's personal account is an unsecured loan to a stranger. Your remedy if it goes wrong is litigation in a Chinese court, in Chinese, over a period measured in years. The escrow account exists so that you never need that remedy.
:::

## The verification sequence

Do these in order. Each one can end the transaction cheaply.

:::checklist
1. Title search at the district registry — ownership, mortgages, seals, objections
2. Seller identity and authority — is the person signing able to sell it
3. Marital consent — a spouse's signature is required where the property is jointly held
4. Planning and use — does the registered use match what you are buying
5. Outstanding fees — management, utilities, heating, property tax position
6. Contract registration — locks the unit and formally tests your eligibility
7. Tax assessment and payment — produces the receipts that release funds
8. Transfer and certificate — the moment the property is yours
9. Handover — inspection, meters, keys, retention released
:::

The first five are done by a lawyer, not by an agent, and cost a fraction of a percentage point of the price. The last four are mechanical if the first five were clean.

## The clauses that decide who carries risk

Seven clauses do almost all of the work. Read them before the deposit, not after.

**Deposit refund trigger.** Title defect, failed eligibility, declined financing — each with a deadline measured in days. Without this clause, the deposit is at the seller's discretion.

**Seller breach penalty.** Double the deposit, payable from escrow rather than by negotiation.

**Date certain for registration.** With a consequence if the seller stalls. Sellers stall when they are trying to find a better offer.

**Vacant possession date.** And who pays management fees in the gap.

**Outstanding fees cap.** A deduction from the balance if the seller's arrears exceed an agreed figure.

**Fixtures and fittings schedule.** Photographed, itemised, signed.

**Price and area reconciliation.** What happens if the registered area differs from the contract.

## The five requests that should end it

Each of these is common enough that people rationalise them. Each is a reliable signal.

:::kill
**"Pay the deposit to my account, the paperwork takes a few days."** There is no reason a seller needs your money before escrow, except that escrow would stop them spending it.

**"We will register a lower price to save tax."** It reduces what you can repatriate later, creates a file that does not reconcile, and is fraud. The money you save is not worth the chain you break.

**"The mortgage can be released after you pay — the bank just needs a few days."** A property under an existing mortgage cannot transfer cleanly. This is a title problem dressed as timing.

**"My spouse is abroad, but her consent is not needed."** For jointly held property it is needed, and it is checked at registration.

**"We can skip the notary, we have known each other for years."** Notarisation exists for the case where someone later says they did not understand what they signed.

## What a clean closing looks like

Boring. Documents arrive when they are asked for, the escrow release conditions are met in order, the tax receipts are issued, the certificate is produced, and nobody asks you for anything unusual. A transaction that feels urgent is a transaction being rushed for someone else's benefit.

If the closing is being accelerated, the correct response is not to speed up — it is to ask what the acceleration is for.

## When to stop

:::checklist
- The title search is not clean, or is "coming tomorrow" — stop
- Anyone asks for money outside escrow — stop
- The registered price is being reduced — stop
- The seller cannot produce a spouse's consent — stop
- The contract has no refund trigger on the deposit — stop
- You are being asked to sign a Chinese contract you have not had translated — stop
:::

This is the last layer. If you have come through all five with a decision rather than an assumption at each one, the purchase is as protected as a foreign buyer can make it.

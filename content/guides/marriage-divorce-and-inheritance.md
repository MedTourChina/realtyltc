:::answer
Ownership in China is not the same as the name on the certificate. Marriage creates a joint claim on anything bought during it; divorce converts that claim into something that must be resolved before a sale; inheritance transfers the whole bundle, claims included, to people who may be in three different countries. If you are buying while married, the question worth asking on day one is not "can I buy" but "who has to sign when I sell".
:::

## The certificate is evidence, not the whole truth

The 不动产权证 (bùdòngchǎn quánzhèng, property ownership certificate) names the registered owner. It is the strongest single piece of evidence about who owns a flat, and for most transactions that is all that matters. It is not a complete statement of everyone with a legal interest.

Three things can sit behind a single name:

- A spouse's marital claim, arising automatically from the date of marriage rather than from anything written on the certificate.
- A mortgage, which gives the bank a say in any transfer until it is discharged.
- A registered 异议登记 (yìyì dēngjì, objection registration) or a court 查封 (cháfēng, seizure), which blocks dealing even though the owner's name is unchanged.

This is why the due diligence step is a search of the register, not a reading of the certificate. The certificate tells you who claims to own it. The register tells you who else has a claim.

## The one-home rule counts a household, not a person

This catches foreign buyers married to Chinese nationals more often than any other rule.

Purchase limits are applied to a 家庭 (jiātíng, household), which means the buyer, the spouse and any minor children. The relevant question at the housing bureau is not "does this person own a home in this city" but "does this household already hold the permitted number of homes here".

The practical consequences:

| Your situation | What the bureau actually checks |
| --- | --- |
| Foreign buyer, foreign spouse, neither owns in the city | Your own eligibility: residence permit, one year of work or study, the 境外个人 one-home cap |
| Foreign buyer, Chinese spouse who already owns a flat in the city | The household already holds a home. In most limited cities this ends the conversation before your own eligibility is examined |
| Foreign buyer, Chinese spouse who owns a flat in another city | Usually irrelevant in cities that count locally, decisive in cities that count nationally — and the two lists change |
| Both names on the certificate | The household still counts one home; the second name does not create a second allowance |

:::warn
Marrying into an existing flat does not give you a purchase allowance, and buying does not retroactively put you on an existing certificate. If you want to be an owner of a home your spouse already holds, that is a 加名 (jiāmíng, adding a name) registration — a different procedure, with its own tax and mortgage consequences, not a purchase.
:::

## What marriage does to ownership

Under the Civil Code, property acquired during the marriage is generally 夫妻共同财产 (fūqī gòngtóng cáichǎn, marital community property) — including a home bought in one spouse's name alone with income earned during the marriage. Pre-marital property, and property given or bequeathed to one spouse specifically as that spouse's own, stays separate.

Two practical points follow, and they point in opposite directions:

1. **If you are the foreign spouse and the flat is in your name only, your Chinese spouse may still have a claim on it.** That claim is invisible on the certificate and only becomes visible in a divorce or a sale.
2. **If the flat is in your spouse's name only and was bought before the marriage, you may have no claim at all** — even if you have lived there for a decade and paid for the renovation.

A written 夫妻财产约定 (fūqī cáichǎn yuēdìng, marital property agreement) can change either outcome. It has to be in writing, it is enforceable between the spouses, and a notarised version carries far more weight with a bank or a registration office than an informal one. If you are buying with money that came from outside China and you want that money traceable on the way out, the agreement is also the document that establishes whose money it was.

## Divorce: the certificate has to be unwound before the flat can move

A divorce does not automatically change the register. Until the property is dealt with, the flat stays exactly as registered, and any sale needs the cooperation of whoever is named.

There are three routes, and they differ enormously in cost:

- **Agreement.** Both parties sign a 离婚协议 (líhūn xiéyì, divorce agreement) specifying who takes the flat and who compensates whom. The registration office then processes 减名 (jiǎnmíng, removing a name) or a transfer on production of the agreement and both parties' presence.
- **Court judgment or mediation statement.** If there is no agreement, a court divides the property. The 判决书 (pànjuéshū, judgment) or 调解书 (tiáojiěshū, mediation statement), once effective, can be used to force the registration without the other party's signature.
- **Doing nothing.** Common, and usually the most expensive option, because the flat becomes unsaleable for as long as the dispute is unresolved, while the mortgage and the property fee keep running.

:::warn
If there is a mortgage, the bank is a party to this whether or not it is in the room. Removing a name from a mortgaged title normally requires the lender's consent, and many lenders require the loan to be repaid or refinanced first. Budget for the possibility that the divorce cannot be completed on the title until the mortgage is cleared — which means finding the cash to clear it.
:::

A foreign spouse divorcing in China should expect the court to require a translation of the marriage certificate and, if the marriage took place abroad, an authenticated version of it. Proceedings conducted in a foreign court are not automatically effective over a flat in China; the judgment generally has to be recognised before a Chinese registration office will act on it.

## Inheritance: three routes, one register

Inheritance in China runs in a statutory order — spouse, children and parents first; siblings and grandparents only if there are none of the first order. A will can change the distribution but not the fact that the registration office needs a documented basis before it will transfer title.

In practice there are three routes to 继承登记 (jìchéng dēngjì, inheritance registration):

| Route | What it involves | Where it fails |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 继承权公证 (notarised succession) | A notary in the city where the property sits verifies the family tree, the will if any, and the heirs' identities, then issues a certificate | Every heir must appear or be represented by a notarised power of attorney. One uncontactable heir stops everything |
| Court judgment | Heirs sue each other or apply for confirmation; the court's ruling is then registered | Slow, adversarial, and it requires at least one party willing to sue |
| Direct registration with verification | Some cities now accept an application without notarisation, publishing a notice and checking the documents themselves | Rules vary by district and change; not available everywhere |

The document chain that has to be assembled, in every route:

- 死亡证明 (sǐwáng zhèngmíng, death certificate), and the 户籍注销证明 (hùjí zhùxiāo zhèngmíng, household registration cancellation) if the deceased was a Chinese national.
- 亲属关系证明 (qīnshǔ guānxì zhèngmíng, proof of kinship) — the hardest one for families that emigrated decades ago. A household register, a personnel file, or a notarised family declaration.
- The property certificate, and a fresh register search showing no mortgage or seizure.
- For heirs abroad: passport, and their own documents authenticated.

Since November 2023 China is a party to the Hague Apostille Convention, so documents from member states need an apostille rather than consular legalisation. Documents from non-member states still go through the embassy. Everything in a foreign language needs a translation by an agency the local office accepts.

:::note
Inheritance is not a purchase. It does not consume the heir's 境外个人 one-home allowance, and it does not require a residence permit or a year of work or study. A foreign heir can inherit a flat in a city they have never lived in.
:::

## The tax you did not expect: selling what you inherited

Inheriting is comparatively cheap. 法定继承人 (fǎdìng jìchéngrén, statutory heirs) are exempt from deed tax on inherited residential property; a non-statutory beneficiary taking under a will is treated as receiving a gift and pays deed tax, generally at 3%.

Selling is where it changes. Two conditions that Chinese sellers rely on may not be available to an heir:

- **满五唯一 (mǎn wǔ wéi yī, five years and the only home)** exempts the seller from individual income tax. The five-year clock generally runs from the original purchase, which an heir can usually inherit. The "only home" test, however, is applied to the heir's own household — so an heir who already owns a flat loses the exemption even for a property held for thirty years.
- **增值税 (zēngzhíshuì, VAT)** is normally exempt after two years of holding, measured from the original purchase date.

Where the exemption is lost, individual income tax is 20% of the gain or 1% of the price, whichever the local office applies. On a flat bought in 2005 and sold in 2026, the gain is nearly the whole price, and the difference between the two methods is enormous.

## The part nobody plans for: getting the money out

This is the real reason to think about inheritance before it happens.

China does not restrict inheriting. It restricts moving the proceeds across the border. The rule that governs it is not about the flat at all — it is about the provenance of the money. Funds that entered through a bank, with a documented purchase and a 完税证明 (wánshuì zhèngmíng, tax payment certificate), can generally leave the same way. Funds with no paper trail cannot, however legitimate the inheritance.

For an heir abroad there are two separate regimes:

1. **继承财产转移 (jìchéng cáichǎn zhuǎnyí, inheritance asset transfer).** An application to the local branch of the foreign exchange administration, for a person who has emigrated or holds foreign nationality, covering the proceeds of an inheritance. It is a one-off approval with a substantial documentary requirement — succession certificate, identity documents, tax clearance, and evidence of how the property was acquired.
2. **The annual US$50,000 facility.** The convenient route for small amounts. It does not scale, and using it to move a property's proceeds in instalments over many years is exactly the pattern that draws scrutiny.

If the flat is sold by an heir who lives in China and the money is then to be sent to relatives abroad, the payment is a 赠予 (zèngyǔ, gift) or a family transfer, with its own documentation and its own limits.

:::checklist
Before you rely on an inheritance, establish these facts — not after the death, now:

- Is there a will, and where is the original? A photocopy is worth very little at a notary's desk.
- How many heirs are in the first order, and in how many countries do they live?
- Is there a marriage certificate for the deceased's marriage, and a divorce certificate if there was an earlier one? Step-children change the heir list.
- Was the flat bought with money that entered China through a bank? Find the records while the person who made the transfer is still alive to explain them.
- Is there a mortgage or a 查封 on the title?
- Does any heir already own a home? That determines whether 满五唯一 will survive the transfer.
:::

## When to walk away

:::kill
Walk away from an inheritance you are counting on, or a marital claim you are relying on, in these situations:

- **Multiple heirs across multiple jurisdictions and no will.** The notarisation route requires every heir to appear or be represented, and the court route requires someone willing to sue their own family. Both can stall for years, and the flat cannot be sold in the meantime.
- **One heir cannot be located or refuses to cooperate.** There is no administrative shortcut. This is a court case, not a paperwork problem.
- **The original purchase documents are gone.** Without them, the tax basis on a sale defaults to something punitive, and the foreign-exchange application has nothing to stand on.
- **The claim depends on a foreign divorce or a foreign will that has never been recognised in China.** Recognition is a separate proceeding with its own timeline.
- **You are being asked to buy into a property that is mid-dispute.** An 异议登记 or a pending lawsuit on the title does not resolve because you are a good-faith buyer. The register will simply refuse to transfer.
:::

The honest summary is that marriage and inheritance are the two ways a Chinese flat acquires owners who never chose each other, and the exit has to satisfy all of them. Buying is a transaction with a counterparty. Selling an inherited or jointly held flat is a negotiation with a family.

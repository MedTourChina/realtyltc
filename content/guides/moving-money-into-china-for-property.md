:::answer
Send the money from your own account, to your own account, with a declared purpose and a complete paper trail. The quota you have heard about applies to buying foreign currency inside China — not to money coming in.
:::

This is the part of a Chinese purchase where otherwise careful buyers make decisions they would never make at home. The reason is that the process is genuinely awkward, the bank staff are cautious, and someone will always offer a shortcut. The shortcut is the risk.

## First, the misunderstanding

The widely quoted USD 50,000 annual figure is the limit on a domestic resident converting RMB into foreign currency. It has nothing to do with money arriving in China.

Inbound remittance into your own Chinese bank account is not capped in the same way. What is controlled is **what happens next**: converting those funds into RMB for the specific purpose of buying a property, and demonstrating that the funds are yours and the purchase is permitted.

## The compliant path

1. **Open a Chinese bank account in your own name**, with a branch that has handled foreign purchase files before. This is the step people skip, and it is the one that determines whether the rest is straightforward.
2. **Remit from an account in your own name abroad.** Third-party remittances — a friend, a relative, your employer's offshore entity — create a source-of-funds problem you will struggle to resolve later.
3. **Declare the purpose accurately** at the remitting bank: purchase of a self-use residential property in China. Do not describe it as a gift, a loan or family support.
4. **Present the supporting file** at the receiving bank: the signed purchase contract, the online contract registration record, your passport and residence permit, and the eligibility confirmation.
5. **Convert in one documented movement** where possible, rather than a series of small transfers.
6. **Retain everything.** SWIFT confirmations, conversion receipts, the contract, the tax receipts and the title certificate. This file is what makes repatriation possible years later.

:::warn
Never let the money sit in a third party's account "just for a few days". Once funds pass through an account that is not yours, the source-of-funds chain is broken, and a bank that cannot trace the chain will not convert the money for a property purchase — regardless of who you are.
:::

## What the bank may additionally ask for

Requirements vary by city and by bank, and they change. Expect some of the following:

:::checklist
- Passport, valid work-type residence permit, and the foreigner work permit
- The purchase contract and the online registration (网签) record
- The district housing bureau's confirmation of your eligibility
- Proof of the source of funds abroad — sale of a previous home, salary history, or an account statement
- A declaration that you hold no other residential property in China
- Tax residency information, and in some cases a self-certification for CRS purposes
:::

Some banks will want the property purchase to be registered before they convert. That creates a sequencing problem — you need RMB to complete, and the bank wants the completed contract to release the RMB. The practical solution is a supervised escrow account and a bank that has done this before, which is a strong argument for choosing your bank early rather than at the last minute.

## The shortcuts, and why they fail

**Using a friend's or relative's quota.** Structuring a purchase through another person's annual conversion allowance is a regulatory breach, and it leaves the funds legally attributable to someone else. When you sell, you cannot demonstrate that the money was yours.

**Splitting into many small transfers.** Structuring to avoid scrutiny is itself the thing that triggers it. Banks see the pattern, and a frozen account at the wrong moment can cost you the deposit.

**Underground or "private" exchange channels.** Illegal foreign exchange dealing is a criminal matter in China, not a compliance footnote. There is no recourse if the money disappears, and there is a real risk of account closure and investigation.

**Crypto conversion.** Not a lawful route for property purchase funds, and it leaves you with no bank-verifiable chain at all.

## Getting the money back out

Repatriation is possible and routine, provided the inbound trail exists. On sale you will normally need:

- The original purchase contract and title certificate
- Tax payment receipts for the sale
- The sale contract and the proceeds record
- Your passport and the same bank relationship

The bank then remits the net proceeds abroad under the documented capital account route. Buyers who arrive with a clean file get paid in weeks. Buyers who arrived through a third party spend months, sometimes years, and occasionally never resolve it.

:::note
If you are buying with a mortgage and your own cash, document both. The loan disbursement has its own trail; your equity contribution needs to be equally traceable, because the repatriation calculation is based on the total.
:::

## A practical sequence

:::checklist
- Open the Chinese account and confirm the branch has done foreign-buyer purchases
- Get pre-approval if borrowing, and confirm the cash requirement including costs
- Sign and register the contract, then open the supervised escrow account
- Remit in one movement from your own account with the purpose declared
- Convert with the full document file, and archive every receipt
- Keep a digital copy of the whole chain outside China as well
:::
